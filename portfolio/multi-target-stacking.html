Multi-Target Stacking

Instead of training different models on one target, I train models on several related targets and use whether they agree to predict the one I actually care about.

ML EngineeringTime SeriesEnsemble Methods
01
Working
on

Standard stacking trains several different learners on one target and combines them. All of the diversity sits in the algorithms. This project moves it to the other side of the problem: vary what the model is asked to predict, and combine those.

What started it was a pattern I kept running into. A better base model earns a higher mean PR-AUC and charges a wider month-to-month standard deviation for it. The two travel together, so you end up with something good on average that you cannot trust in any particular month.

The test bed is intraday breakout prediction on SPY, minute-level. I chose financial data because it is time series, so the temporal structure is real and cannot be shuffled away, and because it is noisy, which is the condition the method has to survive to be worth anything.

02
The
problem

Watch the first thirty minutes of the trading day and stop. Take the close of that thirtieth minute bar as the reference price. The question is whether the day's high, across all the hours that remain, will finish more than 0.4% above it.

One decision per day at a hard information cutoff. Everything up to 10:00 is available and nothing after it is, and nothing updates as the session unfolds. That gives one labelled observation per trading day, a small dataset by the standards of minute-level finance. The breakout rate itself also drifts month to month, which is why evaluation is by PR-AUC rather than ROC-AUC: ROC-AUC is invariant to that drift and reports a flatteringly stable number straight through it.

03
The
idea

The thresholds are nested.

The day's high is a running maximum, so a day that clears +0.5% has necessarily already cleared +0.4%, and a day that clears +0.4% has already cleared +0.3%. Those are not three separate questions. They are three cuts through one quantity, and every day in the dataset carries an answer to all of them at once. The neighbouring labels are free, and committing to a single cutoff throws them away.

So keep them. Train base models at each cut, +0.3%, +0.4%, +0.5%, and -0.4% on the other side of the day's range. Each sees the same features, built from everything available at the cutoff, and returns a probability that its own threshold gets cleared.

I train a large pool of candidates and keep only the ones that perform on the target in question. That selection is where the diversity comes from, and it favours strength over steadiness: a base model that is right most of the time is worth having even if it is streaky, because holding it steady is the second stage's job. A model that does well at +0.3% does not necessarily do well at +0.4%, so the set surviving at one threshold is not the set surviving at the next.

A confident call at +0.4% means more when the models trained on the neighbouring thresholds say the same thing.

So nothing is averaged before the second stage. Every surviving model's prediction goes through individually, because pooling them first would flatten exactly the disagreement that is worth reading. The second stage sees those probabilities and nothing else, never the market data, and what it has to find in them are interactions: the useful pattern is conditional rather than additive, since a confident call at +0.4% counts for more when the +0.5% models back it and for less when they do not. That is a product, not a sum, which is why I expect a tree-based meta-learner to outperform a logistic regression here.

04
The
plan
Doing
Building the framework outlined above: leakage-safe end to end, evaluated by walk-forward validation.
Expecting
A live demo running on pre-calculated predictions, so the results can be poked at rather than only read.
Products
A package for anyone with minute-level data to run the method on their own.
